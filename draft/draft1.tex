\begin{abstract}
The H-2A temporary agricultural guest-worker program requires employers to pay
at least the statutory Adverse Effect Wage Rate (AEWR), which binds on roughly
85--97 percent of certified hours. Historically, the AEWR was set across only
17 Farm Labor Survey (FLS) regions, imposing a severe small-cluster
power ceiling on panel designs. We exploit the October 2, 2025 Interim Final
Rule, which abruptly replaced regional FLS schedules with statewide OEWS
skill-tier schedules, generating statutory wage cuts averaging $-\$3.74$/hr
nationally alongside sharp within-former-region variation across 46 states. Using complete FY2026 Q4 disclosures
and exact delete-one-state jackknife inference, we establish three main
findings. First, offered wages respond immediately and strongly: continuing
filers pass through $\$0.623$ per statutory dollar (95\% CI $[0.422, 0.824]$),
with $\$0.442$ ($[0.205, 0.679]$) within multi-state filers and flat
FY2024--FY2025 placebos, alongside suggestive but specification-sensitive
declines in interim QCEW crop weekly earnings. Second, one-season certified
labor demand is tightly bounded as inelastic: size-weighted PPML certified
hours yield a statutory elasticity of $-0.078$ (95\% CI $[-0.598, +0.443]$,
ruling out unit-elastic demand), with direct-farm growers bounded even more
tightly at $-0.066$ ($[-0.440, +0.308]$). Across five mechanism panels with
Holm multiplicity control, this rigidity is not driven by administrative
denials---requested hours and approval rates are flat---nor offset by contract
duration or weekly-hours expansion. Third, applying the envelope theorem across
all 3,108 contiguous U.S.\ counties shows that short-run AEWR changes operate
overwhelmingly as direct wage transfers: a $+\$1$/hr increase reduces
certification-space farm quasi-rent by $\$209.7$M to $\$205.0$M per year over
2011--2024 ($\$379.3$M to $\$372.6$M in 2024 nominal dollars, with under
2.3\% second-order behavioral cushioning), while the October 2, 2025 cut raises
static 2024 quasi-rent by $\$1.42$B to $\$1.53$B, scaled linearly by the
realized paid-hours ratio $\rho$. A companion power audit of 2011--2025 county
panels explains why pre-reform designs cannot pin down moderate elasticities.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The H-2A temporary agricultural worker program is the only uncapped guest-worker
channel in U.S.\ immigration policy. Certified H-2A employment expanded nearly
fivefold over the past fifteen years, rising from 86.0 million certified hours
in 2011 to 392.5 million hours in 2024 (Figure~\ref{fig:ts_h2a_workers_certified}
traces the 2008--2022 surge in certified positions). Unlike conventional
statutory minimum wages that bind only in the lower tail of a local labor
market, the H-2A Adverse Effect Wage Rate (AEWR) is an occupation-wide wage
floor that binds across nearly the entire program: between 85 and 97 percent of
comparable certified hours are offered within five cents of the applicable
statutory rate. Understanding how agricultural employers adjust wages, labor
demand, and margins when the AEWR changes is central to evaluating farm-labor
policy, domestic spillover debates, and the incidence of guest-worker
regulation.

Estimating the causal effect of the AEWR has proved unusually difficult because
of its institutional geography. From 1987 through September 2025 (outside a
brief 2009--2010 interruption), the U.S.\ Department of Labor (DOL) derived
field-and-livestock AEWRs from the USDA Farm Labor Survey (FLS) across just 17
regional groupings, most of which pool multiple contiguous states under a
single uniform wage floor. Across 2011--2025, universal binding within regions
combined with 17 regional policy nodes creates a structural power wall:
county-panel designs that absorb common regional shocks either lose virtually
all identifying variation or lack enough independent policy clusters to
distinguish zero from economically large elasticities.

On October 2, 2025, DOL issued an Interim Final Rule (IFR) that abruptly
replaced the 17 regional FLS schedules with statewide Occupational Employment
and Wage Statistics (OEWS) skill-tier schedules \citep{dol_2025_ifr}. Overnight,
states that had shared identical AEWRs for decades received starkly different
statutory wage cuts, averaging $-\$3.74$/hr across the contiguous United States
(hours-weighted relative to the 2025 FLS rate) and generating substantial
within-former-region variation across 46 states in the 15 multi-state former FLS regions. Using the complete FY2026 Q4
Office of Foreign Labor Certification (OFLC) disclosure alongside cached
Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW) and Cropland Data Layer (CDL)
records, this paper leverages that quasi-experimental shock to measure
first-stage wage pass-through, bound the short-run quantity response, test five
classes of economic mechanisms, and compute tight partial-equilibrium producer
incidence bounds under the envelope theorem.

Our analysis yields three connected findings. First, statutory AEWR cuts pass
through rapidly and substantially into H-2A wage offers. Comparing continuing
filer--state--occupation cells between FY2025 and FY2026 within former AEWR
regions, each statutory dollar cut reduces nominal hourly wage offers by
$\$0.623$ (95\% state-jackknife CI $[0.422, 0.824]$ across 13,463 cells in 46
states). The pass-through holds under fixed baseline occupation composition
($\$0.620$), across alternative filer linkage tiers ($\$0.621$--$\$0.624$),
within multi-state filers operating across state lines ($\$0.442$, 95\% CI
$[0.205, 0.679]$), and on a county-level first stage ($\$0.671$, SE $0.099$),
while FY2023--FY2024 ($\$0.026$) and FY2024--FY2025 ($\$0.079$) placebos sit
near zero. In interim QCEW private crop-production (NAICS 111) weekly earnings
covering 2025Q4--2026Q1, higher-intensity counties in larger-cut states show a
statistically significant relative earnings decline (implied $-1.97\%$, 95\% CI
$[-3.60\%, -0.30\%]$ at intensity $0.30$ and a $-\$4$ cut), though this
secondary earnings finding is sensitive to employment weighting, exposure
definition, and omitting the baseline intensity control, and cannot separate
wage rates from hours or workforce composition.

Second, despite large immediate wage reductions, one-season H-2A labor
utilization is sharply bounded as inelastic. Across all 2,921 positive-farm-employment
counties in the 15 multi-state former regions, fixed-effects Poisson
pseudo-maximum likelihood (FE-PPML) estimates of complete FY2026 Q4 certified
hours imply a statutory AEWR elasticity of $-0.078$ (exact delete-one-state
jackknife SE $0.259$, 95\% CI $[-0.598, +0.443]$, MDE $0.72$). Completing Q4
tightens precision by 36 percent relative to matched Q1--Q3 disclosures
($-0.198$, 95\% CI $[-1.004, +0.609]$) and rules out unit-elastic ($-1.0$)
certified-hours responses at 95 percent confidence. Certified positions
($+0.064$, 95\% CI $[-0.312, +0.440]$), direct log-rate specifications
($-0.062$, 95\% CI $[-0.452, +0.328]$), August 3 second-schedule and August 26
litigation cutoffs, minimum-wage controls across all 46 states, and
FY2024--FY2025 placebos ($-0.033$, 95\% CI $[-0.293, +0.228]$) all reinforce
this inelastic lower bound.

To understand why large wage cuts did not spark a first-season hiring surge, we
decompose the Track 1 quantity response across five pre-specified mechanism
families---application pipelines, incumbent capacity versus employer entry,
H-2ALC labor contractors versus fixed-site direct growers, planning lead-time
and predetermined crop capital, and intensive contract margins---applying Holm
step-down multiplicity control across 12 primary hypotheses alongside matched
FY2024--FY2025 placebos. No primary mechanism test survives multiplicity
adjustment ($\min p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.939$). Two structural facts nevertheless
sharpen interpretation. First, inelasticity is not an administrative approval
bottleneck: all-filed requested hours ($+0.058$, 95\% CI $[-0.607, +0.724]$),
requested positions ($+0.162$, 95\% CI $[-0.328, +0.652]$), and certification
approval rates ($-0.0062$ per dollar, 95\% CI $[-0.0228, +0.0104]$) do not
respond to the cut. Second, fixed-site direct growers---who account for 74.7\%
of Track 1 hours (65.9\% nationally)---deliver the tightest elasticity bounds
in the program: $-0.066$ ($[-0.440, +0.308]$, MDE $0.52$) for hours and
$-0.021$ ($[-0.293, +0.251]$, MDE $0.38$) for positions, while intensive
contract duration and weekly hours are tightly zero ($-0.043$ and $+0.016$,
MDE $0.08$ for weekly hours). Late-season spring/summer starts (April--September)
display a larger negative point estimate ($-0.676$, unadjusted $p<0.10$,
$p_{\text{Holm}}=0.939$), but the matched FY2024--FY2025 placebo is also
negative ($-0.286$) and the paired late-minus-early contrast is imprecise.

Third, this empirical lower bound sharply disciplines producer welfare
incidence. Under the envelope theorem, the marginal impact of a wage-floor
change on farm quasi-rent equals minus exposed hours, so behavioral responses
enter only at second order. Evaluating exact partial-equilibrium envelope
integrals across all 3,108 contiguous U.S.\ counties over an elasticity bounds
grid anchored at the FY2026 Q4 Track 1 lower bound ($\eta = -0.60$) demonstrates
that the first-order wage transfer captures more than 97.7 percent of total
producer incidence for a $+\$1$/hr AEWR shift. For an anticipated $+\$1$/hr
real increase over 2011--2024, annual certification-space farm quasi-rent falls
by $\$209.7$M at $\eta=0$ versus $\$205.0$M at $\eta=-0.60$ (a $+2.23\%$
behavioral cushion, representing $0.45\%$ of BEA U.S.\ farm hired-labor
expense); on 2024 nominal hours, the corresponding range is $-\$379.3$M to
$-\$372.6$M ($+1.76\%$ cushion, or $-\$236.2$M to $-\$233.6$M under the
observed $0.623$ offered-wage pass-through). Evaluated statically on 2024
hours, the actual October 2, 2025 statutory cut generates a $+\$1.42$B
first-order transfer to $+\$1.53$B upper envelope gain ($+8.10\%$ second-order
amplification). Every monetary figure is reported transparently in
certification space, so paid-hours incidence equals $\rho$ times the reported
value for any constant realization share $\rho$.

Finally, Section~\ref{sec:pre2025_power} and the Appendix synthesize why
pre-2025 county-panel designs cannot independently pin down moderate AEWR
elasticities. Using a full-panel FE-PPML bite specification over 2011--2025
alongside audits of cross-border county pairs, dissimilarity IV, and correlated
random-effects models, we show how universal within-region AEWR binding,
effective cluster concentration ($G_{\text{eff}} = 13.4$ regions), and sample
or exclusion restrictions limit pre-reform inference---explaining why the
46-state 2025 reform is uniquely informative for short-run H-2A responsiveness
and producer incidence.

\section{Institutional Background and Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{The H-2A Certification Process and Non-Wage Costs}

Created under the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 as the statutory
successor to the wartime Bracero program, H-2A allows U.S.\ agricultural
employers to hire foreign nationals for temporary or seasonal agricultural
work with no numerical cap \citep{dol_h2a_info,usda_h2a_checklist}. Hiring
through H-2A requires a multi-agency certification process that begins roughly
60 to 90 days before the date of need: employers file a job order with their
State Workforce Agency (SWA) and undergo pre-occupancy housing inspection,
apply to DOL's Office of Foreign Labor Certification (OFLC), conduct mandatory
domestic recruitment (interviewing and hiring any qualified U.S.\ applicant),
obtain final labor certification, petition U.S.\ Citizenship and Immigration
Services (USCIS), and coordinate consular visa issuance before workers travel
to the worksite \citep{usda_h2a_checklist}.

Beyond the hourly wage, employers face substantial non-wage fixed and
per-worker obligations: inbound and outbound international transportation,
visa and filing fees, daily worksite and weekly grocery transit, and
SWA-inspected free housing for all H-2A workers (as well as corresponding
domestic workers unable to return to their residences daily)
\citep{usda_h2a_info}. In addition, the ``corresponding employment'' rule
requires H-2A employers to pay domestic workers performing substantially the
same duties at least the same offered hourly wage floor and equivalent housing
and transportation terms. These up-front housing capacity limits, pre-season
filing lead times, and multi-year crop commitments are central to interpreting
why one-season labor demand may respond far less elastically than offered
wages.

\subsection{AEWR Rulemaking and the October 2, 2025 Reform}

Employers must offer H-2A workers the highest of four wage floors: the
applicable AEWR, the prevailing wage, an agreed collective-bargaining rate, or
the federal or state statutory minimum wage \citep{dol_aewr}. In practice, the
AEWR almost always binds across field and livestock jobs. For nearly four
decades (1987--2025), DOL derived non-range field-and-livestock AEWRs from the
prior year's USDA Farm Labor Survey combined field-and-livestock average hourly
wage across 17 regional groupings (15 multi-state regions plus California and
Florida as single-state regions; Alaska, Hawaii, and D.C.\ use separate
schedules). This FLS methodology covered the six major agricultural Standard
Occupational Classification (SOC) codes---45-2041 (graders/sorters), 45-2091
(equipment operators), 45-2092 (crop/nursery/greenhouse farmworkers), 45-2093
(livestock farmworkers), 53-7064 (hand packers), and 45-2099 (all other
agricultural workers)---which account for the vast majority of H-2A hours.

Three regulatory transitions punctuate this regime:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{2009--2010 OES interruption:} A rule effective January 17, 2009
    briefly replaced FLS regional rates with area-by-occupation-by-skill OES
    wages; following litigation \citep{ncga_v_solis_2009}, DOL restored FLS
    rates effective March 15, 2010 \citep{dol_2010_fr}. Because canonical
    job-level 2009 statutory determinations are not published in uniform state
    schedules (Table 1 of 75 FR 6884 reports application means rather than
    statutory schedules), our repository leaves canonical 2009 AEWR missing.
  \item \emph{2023 occupation-specific disaggregation:} Effective March 30,
    2023, DOL retained FLS rates for the six combined field-and-livestock
    occupations while moving non-core occupations (such as trucking,
    construction, and supervisors) to state-by-occupation OEWS rates
    \citep{dol_2023_fr}.
  \item \emph{October 2, 2025 statewide OEWS skill-tier IFR:} Following USDA's
    suspension of the Farm Labor Survey, DOL issued an Interim Final Rule
    effective October 2, 2025 replacing regional FLS field-and-livestock AEWRs
    with statewide OEWS-based skill-tier schedules (Skill Level I and Skill
    Level II) alongside a separate H-2A adverse-effect housing adjustment
    \citep{dol_2025_ifr}. Crucially, states within the same former FLS region
    had faced identical statutory AEWRs through FY2025 but received sharply
    different statewide rates in FY2026: across the 48 contiguous states, the
    October 2, 2025 U.S.-worker Skill-I rate cut the FY2025 FLS rate by an
    hours-weighted average of $-\$3.74$/hr (unweighted mean $-\$3.96$/hr, SD
    $\$1.22$/hr; across the 46 states in the 15 multi-state former regions, the
    unweighted mean cut is $-\$3.98$/hr, SD $\$1.24$/hr, and the 2024
    hours-weighted cut is $-\$3.77$/hr). On August 3, 2026,
    DOL published a second annual statewide OEWS schedule, and on August 26,
    2026 federal litigation (\emph{UFW v.\ DOL}) vacated the IFR with backpay
    questions beginning September 2, 2026. Our empirical design explicitly
    audits all receipt and decision cutoffs around August 3 and August 26.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conceptual Framework and the Envelope Theorem}

Consider a price-taking farm operation choosing H-2A certified hours $H^c$ and
other inputs (domestic labor, land, machinery, and pre-installed housing
capacity $\bar{K}$) facing an anticipated statutory AEWR $a$. Let $X(a) \equiv
x \cdot H^c(a)$ denote AEWR-exposed certified hours, where $x \in (0, 1]$ is
the fraction of certified hours for which the AEWR is the active wage margin,
and let $\pi \in (0, 1]$ capture the pass-through from statutory AEWR shifts
$\Delta a$ to effective hourly offered wages ($\Delta_{\text{eff}} = \pi \Delta
a$). Partial pass-through ($\pi < 1$) arises naturally when some jobs sit above
the floor due to competing state minimum wages, prevailing wages, Skill-II
classifications, multi-occupation bundles, or employer retention premia.

In the short run (a single agricultural season), quasi-fixed constraints---such
as SWA-inspected housing capacity $\bar{K}$, pre-season recruitment and
crop-planting commitments, and perennial orchard acreage---limit how far
employers can expand H-2A hours even when the statutory wage floor falls
sharply. Letting $\Pi(a) = \max_{H^c, z} \{p F(\rho H^c, z; \bar{K}) - \rho
w(a) H^c - c(z)\}$ denote short-run partial-equilibrium farm quasi-rent
(holding output prices $p$ and non-H-2A domestic wages fixed), where $\rho \in
(0, 1]$ is a constant ratio mapping certified hours to realized paid hours, the
envelope theorem yields:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d \Pi(a)}{da} = -\rho \, \pi \, X(a).
  \label{eq:envelope_marginal}
\end{equation}
Integrating \eqref{eq:envelope_marginal} across a finite anticipated AEWR
change from $a_0$ to $a_1 = a_0 + \Delta_{\text{eff}}$ gives exact
certification-space producer incidence ($\rho = 1$):
\begin{equation}
  \Delta \Pi^{PE} = -\int_{a_0}^{a_0 + \Delta_{\text{eff}}} X(s)\,ds
  = \underbrace{-X_0 \Delta_{\text{eff}}}_{\text{First-order transfer }\Delta\Pi^{PE}(0)}
  + \underbrace{\left[\Delta\Pi^{PE} - \Delta\Pi^{PE}(0)\right]}_{\text{Second-order behavioral cushion } \ge 0}.
  \label{eq:envelope_integral}
\end{equation}
Under a local constant-elasticity certified-hours curve
$H^c(a) = H^c_0 (a/a_0)^\eta$ with $\eta = d\log H^c / d\log a \le 0$,
\eqref{eq:envelope_integral} evaluates analytically to
\begin{equation}
  \Delta \Pi^{PE}(\eta) =
  \begin{cases}
    -X_0 a_0 \dfrac{(1 + \Delta_{\text{eff}}/a_0)^{\eta + 1} - 1}{\eta + 1}, & \eta \neq -1, \\[8pt]
    -X_0 a_0 \ln\!\left(1 + \dfrac{\Delta_{\text{eff}}}{a_0}\right), & \eta = -1.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:ce_formula}
\end{equation}
Convexity of the profit function guarantees that the second-order behavioral
term is nonnegative for both wage increases (mitigating producer losses as
farms cut back hours) and wage cuts (amplifying producer gains as farms expand
hours), and is proportional to $|\eta| \cdot |\Delta_{\text{eff}}/a_0| / 2$.
Consequently, once an empirical design establishes a credible lower bound on
$\eta$ (ruling out strongly elastic responses), producer incidence is tightly
pinned down even when $\eta$ itself is not estimated to two decimal places.

\section{Data and Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Data Sources and Panel Construction}

Our primary microdata come from DOL OFLC H-2A public disclosure workbooks for
fiscal years 2008 through complete FY2026 Q4 (decisions through September 30,
2026). Worksites are geocoded to 2010-vintage 5-digit string county FIPS codes
using a tiered ZIP, Census place, fuzzy-match, and cached geocoding pipeline,
with multi-county applications allocated equally across worksite counties and
certified contract hours conserving total mass across calendar and fiscal
years. We pair two distinct entity linkages:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Filer-level linkage (R7, with R5, R8, and FEIN
    sensitivities):} Used in the offered-wage pass-through regressions to track
    continuing filing entities across FY2023--FY2026 within state-occupation
    cells. Filing entities include both direct farms and H-2A labor contractors
    (H-2ALCs) and are explicitly distinct from physical worksite businesses.
  \item \emph{Worksite-business linkage (\texttt{employer\_id\_balanced}, v2):}
    Constructed over FY2008--FY2026 Q4 disclosures (manual review of the FY2026
    Q4 extension pending) to identify distinct worksite businesses for the
    mechanism decomposition between incumbent and new-entrant employers while
    avoiding the FY2020 Addendum B disclosure break across FY2021--FY2026.
\end{itemize}

Statutory wage schedules combine CRS historical FLS rates, cached Federal
Register notices, and the October 2, 2025 / August 3, 2026 OEWS skill-tier
lookup tables, joined to a unified 51-jurisdiction FRED state and applicable
agricultural minimum-wage panel (\texttt{state\_real\_minwages.parquet}) covering all
46 multi-state-region states through 2026 without missing federal-floor states.
For secondary earnings, crop-capital, and welfare scale checks, we incorporate
cached BLS QCEW quarterly single-file archives through 2026Q1 (private NAICS
111 crop production, NAICS 112 animal production, and all-private wages),
USDA CroplandCROS Cropland Data Layer (CDL) county crop-type shares over
2022--2024 (covering 100\% of our 2,921 Track 1 counties), BEA farm employment
and CAINC45 farm hired-labor expenses and crop cash receipts (2011--2022,
deflated by the WPU01 farm-products PPI, $2012 = 100$), and ACS commuting-zone
wage quantiles. All shared county panels cover the balanced 3,108 contiguous
U.S.\ counties (excluding Alaska, Hawaii, and D.C.).

\subsection{Identification, Inference, and Transparency}

Our 2025 policy-shift analyses exploit within-former-FLS-region dispersion in
the October 2, 2025 statewide statutory AEWR across the 46 states belonging to
the 15 multi-state former AEWR regions (California and Florida are absorbed as
single-state former regions).

\paragraph{Offered-wage pass-through.}
For continuing R7 filer--state--occupation cell $j$ in state $s$ and former
FLS region $r(s)$, we estimate the two-period first-difference specification
\begin{equation}
  \Delta w_{js} = \pi \, \text{Dose}_s + \lambda_{r(s)} + u_{js},
  \label{eq:wage_reg}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta w_{js} = \bar{w}_{js,\text{FY26}} - \bar{w}_{js,\text{FY25}}$ is
the nominal hourly wage offer change over full fiscal-year decisions (October 1
through September 30), $\text{Dose}_s = \text{AEWR}^{\text{US, Skill I}}_{s,\text{Oct 2025}} - \text{AEWR}^{\text{FLS}}_{r(s),\text{Apr 2025}}$ is the
statutory dollar cut (negative where rates fell), and $\lambda_{r(s)}$ absorbs
former-region fixed effects. Primary regressions weight by FY2025 baseline
certified hours, paired with equal-weighted, fixed-occupation-composition, and
within-multi-state-filer (adding filer fixed effects) specifications.

\paragraph{County H-2A quantity response and mechanisms.}
Across all 2,921 counties with positive fixed-2011 BEA farm employment in the 15
multi-state former regions, we stack FY2025 and FY2026 full-fiscal-year totals
and estimate size-weighted fixed-effects Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood
(FE-PPML; \citealp{silva_tenreyro_2006}):
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[Y_{ct} \mid p_{st}, \alpha_c, \gamma_{r(c)t}]
  = \exp\!\left(\beta \, p_{st} + \alpha_c + \gamma_{r(c)t}\right),
  \label{eq:qty_ppml}
\end{equation}
where $Y_{ct}$ is county certified hours (or positions, or a mechanism subgroup
count), $p_{st}$ is the statutory rate (the regional FLS rate in FY2025 and the
October 2, 2025 statewide U.S.\ Skill-I rate in FY2026), $\alpha_c$ absorbs
county fixed effects, and $\gamma_{r(c)t}$ absorbs former-region-by-fiscal-year
fixed effects. Because $\gamma_{r(c)t}$ absorbs the national and regional level
shifts, $\beta$ is identified strictly from within-former-region state rate
dispersion. Scaling by the estimation-sample mean statutory rate $\bar{p}$
yields the statutory elasticity $\hat{\eta} = \hat{\beta}\bar{p}$ (verified
against direct $\ln(p_{st})$ PPML). We estimate the exact same model on
FY2024--FY2025 as a pre-policy placebo for every headline and mechanism outcome.

\paragraph{Inference, multiplicity, and post-inspection disclosure.}
Because policy assignment varies at the state level across 46 states (47 in
QCEW interim earnings), primary inference in every specification uses the exact
delete-one-state cluster jackknife (CV3J) with $t(G-1)$ critical values
\citep{mackinnon_nielsen_webb_2023}, alongside conventional state-clustered CRV1
standard errors. Paired subgroup contrasts (such as entrant minus incumbent,
contractor minus direct grower, late minus early starts, and implied intensive
elasticities $\hat{\theta}_H - \hat{\theta}_N$) are computed from paired
46-state jackknife deletion draws. Across the 12 declared primary mechanism
hypotheses, we report Holm step-down multiplicity-adjusted $p$-values
\citep{holm_1979}. In keeping with transparent empirical reporting, we emphasize
that the November 2026 FY2026 Q4 baseline, Track 1 quantity module, and
five-panel mechanism audit were implemented after earlier FY2026 wage and
Q1--Q3 outcomes had been inspected; they are documented via dated amendments
rather than claimed as fresh preregistrations.

\section{Wage Pass-Through and Covered Crop Earnings}
\label{sec:wages}

\subsection{Immediate Offered-Wage Pass-Through}

Table~\ref{tab:policy_2025_wages} and Figure~\ref{fig:policy_2025_wages} present
our first-stage wage-offer results on the complete FY2026 Q4 disclosure. Across
13,463 continuing R7 filer--state--occupation cells in 46 states, a $\$1.00$
larger statutory cut reduces baseline-hours-weighted nominal hourly wage offers
by $\$0.623$ (CV3J SE $\$0.100$, 95\% CI $[0.422, 0.824]$; Panel A, row 1).
Equal cell weighting yields a nearly identical $\$0.600$ ($[0.266, 0.933]$).
Holding each filer--state cell's occupation shares fixed at FY2025 levels
yields $\$0.620$ ($[0.418, 0.822]$ across 12,540 continuing filer--states,
covering 95.9\% of baseline hours), confirming that occupational mix shifts do
not drive the wage decline.

unadjusted $p<0.10$, Holm $p=0.159$), and large filers is statistically
insignificant after Holm multiplicity adjustment.

Two institutional caveats remain essential. First, R7 tracks filing entities
rather than physical farms, and exact job-level effective legal floors cannot
be established with certainty because receipt dates do not observe SWA
job-order submission dates and skill-tier, worker-category, prevailing-wage,
and multi-state itinerary rules interact (by FY2026, 17.6\% of hours in our
schedule-envelope audit sit in states where a competing minimum wage exceeds
the lowest statutory reference). Second, offered wages measure certified
contract offers rather than realized payroll earnings.

\section{Short-Run Quantity Response and Mechanisms}
\label{sec:quantity}

\subsection{Complete FY2026 Q4 County Quantity Bounds}

Having established an immediate $\$0.623$--$\$0.671$ hourly wage pass-through,
we test whether certified H-2A labor utilization expanded in response to lower
statutory wage floors during FY2026. Table~\ref{tab:policy_2025_quantity}
reports Track 1 estimates across all 2,921 positive-farm-employment counties in
the 15 multi-state former AEWR regions (46 states).

\input{../outputs/tables/table_policy_2025_quantity.tex}

Panel A delivers our headline behavioral result. On the complete FY2026 Q4
disclosure (full fiscal year October 1--September 30), the primary
size-weighted FE-PPML regression of county certified hours on the statutory
AEWR yields a slope of $-0.0051$ (CV3J SE $0.0168$), corresponding to a
statutory elasticity of $-0.078$ (95\% state-jackknife CI $[-0.598,
+0.443]$, MDE $0.72$). Direct log-rate PPML yields an even tighter elasticity
of $-0.062$ (95\% CI $[-0.452, +0.328]$, MDE $0.54$), and FE-PPML certified
positions give $+0.064$ (95\% CI $[-0.312, +0.440]$, MDE $0.52$). Equal-weighted
OLS per-2011-worker ratios are positive and noisier ($+0.261$ for hours,
$+0.544$ for positions), while the county-level offered-wage first stage on the
exact same sample is $+0.6714^{***}$ (CV3J SE $0.0988$, 95\% CI $[0.47, 0.87]$).

Panel B confirms both the precision gain from completing Q4 and the robustness
of this bound to legal cutoffs and pre-trends. Moving from matched Q1--Q3
disclosures ($-0.198$, 95\% CI $[-1.004, +0.609]$, MDE $1.12$) to the complete
fiscal year cuts the jackknife standard error by 36 percent and rules out
unit-elastic ($-1.0$) certified-hours demand at the 95 percent confidence
level. Symmetric cutoffs excluding cases decided or received on or after the
August 3, 2026 second schedule ($-0.167$ and $-0.180$) or the August 26, 2026
\emph{UFW} vacatur ($-0.178$ and $-0.113$, CI $[-0.546, +0.320]$) yield closely
aligned inelastic bounds. Matched FY2024--FY2025 pre-policy placebos sit
tightly at zero for both full-FY ($-0.033$, 95\% CI $[-0.293, +0.228]$) and
Q1--Q3 ($-0.079$, 95\% CI $[-0.605, +0.447]$) windows.

Panel C verifies that alternative statutory rates and state controls leave the
bound unchanged across all 46 states: using the H-2A Skill-I cash rate
($-0.081$, $[-0.521, +0.359]$), U.S.\ Skill-II rate ($+0.181$), pre-October 2
receipt cohort ($-0.063$), dual-schedule cohort ($-0.044$), or controlling for
general state minimum wages ($-0.061$) or applicable agricultural minimum wages
($-0.084$, $[-0.603, +0.436]$) reproduces the same narrow interval. Scaling
the statutory elasticity into an implied total-cost elasticity using a central
non-wage cost ratio of $1.2401$ yields $-0.162$ (95\% CI $[-1.246, +0.922]$)
under the R7 pass-through ($0.6229$) and $-0.150$ ($[-1.149, +0.848]$) under
the paired-jackknife county first stage ($0.6714$). We interpret the headline
$-0.078$ ($[-0.598, +0.443]$) strictly as a credible one-season lower bound on
elastic demand rather than a tightly pinned structural point estimate.

across five economic channels---application pipelines, incumbent capacity

lead-time and crop capital, and intensive contract margins---evaluated on the
identical 2,921-county, 46-state frame with exact FY2024--FY2025 placebos and
Holm step-down multiplicity adjustment across 12 declared primary hypotheses.

Across all 12 primary mechanism hypotheses, no estimate is statistically
significant after Holm multiplicity control ($\min p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.939$).
Even so, the five panels provide concrete, falsifiable discipline on competing
explanations of short-run inelasticity:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Not an administrative processing or denial bottleneck (Panel A):}
    In FY2026, Track 1 employers requested 379.5 million hours and 370.2
    million hours were certified (an approval ratio of 97.5\%). Including
    denied and withdrawn applications from raw disclosure records, all-filed
    requested hours have a statutory elasticity of $+0.058$ (95\% CI $[-0.607,
    +0.724]$, $p_{\text{Holm}}=1.000$), requested positions give $+0.162$
    ($[-0.328, +0.652]$), the certification approval rate changes by $-0.0062$
    per dollar ($[-0.0228, +0.0104]$), and the paired requested-minus-certified
    hours difference is $+0.136$ ($[-0.322, +0.594]$). Employers did not file
    a wave of additional requests that OFLC or SWAs subsequently blocked.
  \item \emph{Direct growers anchor the tightest inelastic bound (Panel C):}
    Splitting certified hours between H-2ALC labor contractors (25.3\% of Track 1
    hours; 34.1\% nationally) and fixed-site direct growers (74.7\% of Track 1
    hours; 65.9\% nationally) reveals a sharp precision contrast. Fixed-site
    direct growers exhibit a statutory hours elasticity of $-0.066$ (CV3J
    SE $0.186$, 95\% CI $[-0.440, +0.308]$, MDE $0.52$; placebo $+0.064$) and
    a positions elasticity of $-0.021$ (CV3J SE $0.135$, 95\% CI $[-0.293,
    +0.251]$, MDE $0.38$). H-2ALC contractor hours are far noisier ($-0.103$,
    95\% CI $[-2.417, +2.210]$, with a negative placebo of $-0.602$), and the
    contractor share of county hours is flat ($+0.0016$, SE $0.0044$). Thus,
    direct agricultural employers bound one-season demand more tightly than
    $-0.44$ for hours and $-0.29$ for positions.
  \item \emph{Incumbent capacity dominates short-run volume; entry is noisy
    (Panel B):} Using canonical worksite-business IDs (\texttt{employer\_id\_balanced}),
    incumbent businesses active in the prior fiscal year account for 86.2\% of
    worksite-business-linked FY2026 Track 1 hours (with linked worksite
    businesses covering 85.0\% of all Track 1 hours) and exhibit a statutory
    elasticity of $-0.086$ (95\%
    CI $[-0.866, +0.694]$; placebo $+0.113$). New-entrant business hours
    ($-0.213$, 95\% CI $[-4.275, +3.849]$) and distinct active business counts
    ($+0.444$, 95\% CI $[-1.070, +1.958]$) are too volatile over one season to
    detect entry effects, and the paired entrant-minus-incumbent contrast is
    indistinguishable from zero ($-0.127$, SE $2.370$; R7 filer sensitivity
    $-2.551$, SE $1.537$, placebo $+1.111$).
  \item \emph{Suggestive planning lead-time gradient, zero perennial-crop
    interaction (Panel D):} Because the IFR took effect October 2, 2025,
    early-season winter starts (October--March; 63.1\% of FY2026 Track 1 hours)
    were largely committed prior to the rule and yield an elasticity of
    $+0.055$ ($[-0.992, +1.101]$). Late-season spring and summer starts
    (April--September; 36.9\% of hours), filed months after the new schedules
    were known, yield a larger negative point estimate of $-0.676$ (CV3J SE
    $0.375$, 95\% CI $[-1.431, +0.080]$, unadjusted $p=0.078$,
    $p_{\text{Holm}}=0.939$; receipt-date split $\ge$ January 1 gives $-0.525$
    vs.\ $+0.211$). However, the matched FY2024--FY2025 late-start placebo is
    also negative ($-0.286$, SE $0.150$) and the paired late-minus-early
    difference ($-0.730$, SE $0.820$) is imprecise, so we treat this lead-time
    pattern as suggestive motivation for multi-year FY2027+ follow-up rather
    than confirmed adjustment. Splitting counties by predetermined 2022--2024
    CDL perennial fruit-and-tree-nut share shows no significant differential
    response (continuous within-region interaction $+0.012$, SE $0.400$,
    $p_{\text{Holm}}=1.000$; annual-dominated counties $<p_{75}$ give $+0.020$,
    95\% CI $[-0.499, +0.540]$). An exploratory secondary continuous
    interaction with baseline CDL vegetable share is negative ($-0.230$, JK SE
    $0.047$; placebo $-0.071$, SE $0.053$; upper-quartile subgroup contrast
    $-0.567$, SE $0.531$), though explicitly outside the 12-hypothesis primary
    family.
  \item \emph{No intensive contract stretching (Panel E):} Employers did not
    materially lengthen contracts or weekly schedules in states with larger
    cuts. Paired-jackknife implied $\ln(\text{hours/position})$ equals
    $-0.141$ (95\% CI $-0.503$ to $+0.220$, MDE $0.50$), the mean
    contract-weeks elasticity equals $-0.043$ (95\% CI $-0.304$ to $+0.218$,
    MDE $0.36$), and the observed non-imputed weekly-hours elasticity is
    tightly bounded at $+0.016$ (95\% CI $-0.044$ to $+0.077$, MDE $0.08$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Producer Incidence Bounds Under the Envelope Theorem}
\label{sec:welfare}

Because the complete FY2026 Q4 Track 1 design rules out certified-hours
elasticities more negative than $\eta = -0.60$ at 95\% confidence---and direct
growers tighten that floor further to $-0.44$---the envelope formula
\eqref{eq:ce_formula} pins down static partial-equilibrium producer incidence
within a remarkably narrow band. Rather than pretending that a noisy point
estimate is a known structural parameter, we evaluate a transparent bounds grid
$\eta \in \{0.00, -0.30, -0.75, -1.00\}$ anchored directly to the verified
Track 1 Q4 jackknife 95\% lower bound ($\eta = -0.5985 \approx -0.60$) across
all 3,108 contiguous U.S.\ counties in the shared panel.

\input{../outputs/tables/table_welfare_bounds.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:welfare_bounds} summarizes the exact certification-space
envelope bounds (with all 26 provenance and analytic identities verified in
\texttt{code/welfare/}). Panel A evaluates an anticipated $+\$1.00$/hr AEWR increase
under coverage-adjusted AEWR exposure and full statutory pass-through
($\pi = 1.00$). Over the complete 2011--2024 window (unweighted mean of annual
national totals; 209.67M exposed hours/year in 2012 farm-products-PPI real
dollars), producer quasi-rent falls by $-\$209.7$M/yr at the first-order
inelastic envelope ($\eta = 0.00$) versus $-\$205.0$M/yr at the Track 1 Q4
lower bound ($\eta = -0.60$), a second-order behavioral cushion of just
$+2.23\%$. Even at the rejected unit-elastic benchmark ($\eta = -1.00$),
the loss is $-\$201.9$M/yr ($+3.69\%$ cushion), and replacing constant
elasticity with constant semi-elasticity changes the integral by only
$0.097\%$. Scaled against BEA CAINC45 farm accounts over complete 2011--2022
real years, a $+\$1$/hr real AEWR increase represents $0.45\%$ of total U.S.\
farm hired-labor expense ($0.081\%$ of U.S.\ crop cash receipts) at $\eta = 0$.

Because H-2A utilization more than quadrupled between 2011 and 2024, current
policy stakes are substantially larger than the 14-year average. On the latest
complete year (2024; 379.25M coverage-adjusted exposed hours, hours-weighted
nominal AEWR $\$16.65$/hr), a $+\$1.00$/hr nominal increase reduces
certification-space producer quasi-rent by $-\$379.3$M at $\eta = 0.00$ to
$-\$372.6$M at the Track 1 lower bound ($\eta = -0.60$, second-order
cushion $+1.76\%$), and $-\$368.2$M even at $\eta = -1.00$ ($+2.92\%$
cushion, where the mechanical certification-space H-2A wage-bill change
$\Delta E^c_H$ vanishes identically).

Panel B demonstrates that exposure shares and offered-wage pass-through matter
far more for monetary incidence than curvature over $\eta \in [-0.60, 0]$.
Varying the AEWR-exposed share parametrically across $x \in \{0.85, 0.94,
1.00\}$ of all certified hours spans $-\$333.6$M to $-\$392.5$M at $\eta=0$
($-\$327.8$M to $-\$385.6$M at $\eta=-0.60$). Applying the empirically
estimated FY2026 Q4 offered-wage pass-through ($\pi = 0.623$) scales 2024
nominal coverage-adjusted incidence to $-\$236.2$M ($\eta=0$) through
$-\$233.6$M (Track 1 low, cushion $+1.11\%$). Finally, applying the actual
state-level October 2, 2025 U.S.\ Skill-I statutory cuts relative to 2025 FLS
rates (hours-weighted mean cut $-\$3.74$/hr) statically to 2024 certified hours
in nominal dollars yields a certification-space producer gain of $+\$1,418.2$M
at $\eta = 0.00$ to $+\$1,533.1$M at the Track 1 lower bound ($\eta =
-0.60$, gain amplification $+8.10\%$), or $+\$1,618.0$M at $\eta = -1.00$
(a uniform $+10\%$ real AEWR stress test on 2024 hours yields $-\$545.5$M at
$\eta=0$ to $-\$519.9$M at $\eta=-1.00$, cushion $+4.69\%$).

All monetary figures in Table~\ref{tab:welfare_bounds} are strictly in
certification space and hold farm-gate output prices, domestic wages, and
corresponding-employment hours fixed. If realized paid H-2A hours equal a
constant fraction $\rho \in (0, 1]$ of certified hours, paid-hours farm
producer incidence equals $\rho$ times every reported monetary value. Our
calculation intentionally excludes H-2A foreign worker outside-option surplus,
domestic corresponding-worker transfers, administrative compliance costs, and
multi-year crop-mix or mechanization adjustments.

\section{Why Pre-2025 County Panels Cannot Pin Down Moderate Elasticities}
\label{sec:pre2025_power}

Before the October 2, 2025 statewide shift, empirical research on the AEWR had
to rely on historical variation across the 17 regional FLS schedules. A natural
question is why we rely on the 2025 reform rather than a conventional
2011--2025 county-panel difference-in-differences, local border, or
instrumental-variables design. Across four distinct county-panel designs
audited in this repository, historical pre-2025 data confront two structural
barriers: \emph{universal within-region floor binding} (roughly 94\% of
2011--2013 H-2A hours are paid at the AEWR, leaving little differential wage
``bite'' across counties within a region) and a \emph{17-region effective
cluster ceiling} (with California alone carrying 12.1\% of Fisher-information
weight, reducing effective independent clusters to $G_{\text{eff}} = 13.36$).

First, we audit a full-panel fixed-effects PPML ``bite'' design over all 3,108
contiguous counties in 2011--2025 (\texttt{code/designs/bite}, detailed in
Appendix~\ref{app:pre2025_audit}, Tables~\ref{tab:bite_main}--\ref{tab:bite_diagnostics},
and Figure~\ref{fig:bite_event_study}). Earlier county-panel difference-in-differences
implementations that dropped ``always-taker'' counties (discarding 62.6\% of
U.S.\ H-2A workers), used time-varying local wage gaps, and clustered below the
AEWR region suffer from severe sample selection, mechanical local-wage
endogeneity, and downward-biased standard errors; we retire those specifications
completely from the paper. Fixing every methodological flaw---retaining all
3,108 counties via FE-PPML, freezing baseline exposures strictly over 2008/2010
(respecting missing canonical 2009 AEWR), controlling for predetermined
covariates interacted with year so 2023--2025 survive BEA discontinuation, and
reporting exact delete-one-region CV3J jackknife inference---yields a primary
differential elasticity on $E_{p25} \times \ln(\text{AEWR}_{rt})$ of $+0.003$
(CV3J SE $0.135$, MDE $0.379$, clean 2008--2010 pre-trend $F=0.531, p=0.667$;
Table~\ref{tab:bite_main}). Because Region $\times$ Year effects absorb the
common regional response, this estimand captures only differential bite
heterogeneity across counties within the same region, not an aggregate AEWR
elasticity \citep{callaway_goodman_bacon_santanna_2024}; across a $+2$ SD
exposure contrast, its 95\% CV3J interval spans $[-0.57, +0.58]$, ruling out
massive differential collapses but leaving moderate elasticities unresolved.
Adding $E_{p25} \times \text{Year}$ effects wipes out 96.6\% of residualized
variation (MDE $2.11$). Unlocking all 48 states through unified agricultural
minimum-wage series confirms that domestic-floor regressions on
$\ln(\text{prevailing ag.\ min.\ wage})$ also remain underpowered ($+0.376$,
state CV3J SE $0.374$, MDE $1.05$; Table~\ref{tab:bite_diagnostics}, Panel E).

Second, three alternative pre-2025 county designs hit closely related walls:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Cross-region border county pairs (\citealp{dube_lester_reich_2010}
    approach, \texttt{border\_discontinuity} v7):} Across 807 agriculturally screened
    counties spanning 25 regional border pairs (2011--2025), all 25 pairs share
    only 16 regional nodes (signed incidence rank 15; effective pair count
    11.58). Exact pair-CV3J ($0.39 \pm 4.98$ certifications per 1,000 farm
    workers) and dyadic shared-endpoint standard errors ($5.25$;
    \citealp{aronow_samii_assenova_2015}) fail declared power and size-validity
    gates across 0 of 8 candidate-method pairs (retrospective shared-region
    MDEs exceed 62--76 certifications per 1,000 workers), and weak pre-period
    diagnostics cannot certify parallel trends \citep{roth_2022}.
  \item \emph{Entropy-calibrated dissimilarity Panel IV (\texttt{panel\_iv},
    2011--2023):} Calibrating county donor weights to published FLS moments
    yields a strong statistical first stage ($F = 44.71$ across 80 subregions)
    and negative 2SLS point estimates for normalized certified hours
    ($-65.52$, SE $31.61$; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:iv_h2a_adjustment_margins})
    and workers ($-0.089$, SE $0.049$), but relies on current-year FLS targets
    that can induce mechanical relevance and shares regional economic shocks
    across same-region donors without establishing exclusion.
  \item \emph{Correlated random-effects continuous-dose panels
    (\texttt{mundlak\_chamberlain} v4.1.0, 2012--2022):} Flexible linear-history OLS
    models with 1,261--3,930 effective columns confront extreme leverage
    (maximum full-model leverage $0.84$--$0.97$), high condition numbers
    ($>10^6$), and 17-cluster inference breakdown.
\end{itemize}
Taken together, these four pre-2025 audits show that no amount of county-level
refinement can manufacture independent regional policy assignments out of 17
FLS regions. Breaking the 17-region ceiling required the 46-state within-region
statutory shock of October 2, 2025.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Exploiting the October 2, 2025 transition from regional FLS schedules to
statewide OEWS skill-tier AEWRs provides the sharpest quasi-experimental window
to date into how U.S.\ agricultural employers respond to statutory guest-worker
wage floors. Across 46 states, continuing H-2A filers passed through $\$0.623$
per statutory dollar into nominal hourly offers within the first fiscal year
($\$0.442$ within multi-state filers), accompanied by suggestive---though
specification-sensitive---relative declines in interim QCEW crop weekly
earnings. Yet complete FY2026 Q4 disclosures bound the one-season certified
hours response tightly against elastic expansion: the statutory PPML elasticity
is $-0.078$ (95\% CI $[-0.598, +0.443]$), ruling out unit-elastic demand, with
fixed-site direct growers bounded even more tightly at $-0.066$ ($[-0.440,
+0.308]$) and zero evidence of administrative approval bottlenecks or
intensive-margin hours stretching.

These estimates carry a direct lesson for policy incidence. Under the envelope
theorem, short-run AEWR adjustments act overwhelmingly as direct wage transfers
on inframarginal certified hours: behavioral re-optimization cushions less than
2.3\% of producer surplus losses for a $+\$1$/hr hike ($\$205.0$M--$\$209.7$M/yr
over 2011--2024; $\$372.6$M--$\$379.3$M on 2024 nominal hours) and amplifies
the static gain from the October 2, 2025 cut by at most 8.1\%
($\$1.42$B--$\$1.53$B in certification space, scaled by realized paid-hours
share $\rho$). Because FY2026 captures a single season marked by quasi-fixed
housing, pre-committed crop acreage, and late-year litigation, our bounds
characterize short-run incidence rather than long-run structural elasticities.
Whether the suggestive gradient observed in late-season April--September starts
compounds into larger multi-year adjustments as growers expand housing, alter
crop mixes, or re-enter the program remains a central question for FY2027 and
beyond.
